TermPlainly vs Rocket Lawyer: Which Is Better for Tenants Reviewing a Lease?

Signing a lease without fully understanding it is one of the most common financial mistakes renters make. Both TermPlainly and Rocket Lawyer promise to help — but they take very different approaches, and the right choice depends on what you actually need.

What Each Tool Does

TermPlainly is a document translation tool. You drop in a PDF or DOCX file — your actual lease — and it returns a plain-English breakdown of what the document says. The focus is narrow and deliberate: take dense legalese and make it readable, fast.

Rocket Lawyer is a broad legal platform. It offers document templates, access to licensed attorneys for consultations, and a library of legal guides. For tenants, this means you can read articles about lease law, use their own lease templates, or pay for attorney advice. What it doesn't do natively is analyze your specific lease document and translate it for you.

TermPlainly: Pros and Cons for Tenants

Pros

  • It reads your actual lease. This is the core differentiator. You're not reading a generic article about what leases usually say — you're getting an explanation of your clauses, your penalties, your renewal terms.
  • Extremely fast. Upload and get results in seconds, not hours.
  • Low friction. No account setup maze, no subscription upsell before you can do anything useful.
  • Good for spotting red flags. Unusual clauses, automatic renewal traps, and vague maintenance language tend to surface clearly in plain-English summaries.

Cons

  • Not a lawyer. TermPlainly explains what a clause says, but it can't tell you whether a clause is enforceable in your jurisdiction, or whether your landlord is violating local tenant protection laws.
  • No legal recourse pathway. If you find something alarming in your lease, TermPlainly doesn't connect you to next steps or professional help.
  • Limited scope. It's a single-purpose tool. If you need a broader understanding of your tenant rights, you'll need to look elsewhere.
  • Accuracy depends on document quality. Scanned PDFs or poorly formatted documents may not parse cleanly.

Rocket Lawyer: Pros and Cons for Tenants

Pros

  • Real attorney access. If you have a serious concern — a clause that looks illegal, a landlord dispute already in progress — Rocket Lawyer lets you consult a licensed attorney. This is meaningful, substantive help that no AI translation tool can replicate.
  • Jurisdiction-aware content. Their legal guides frequently address state-specific tenant rights, which matters enormously since lease law varies dramatically by location.
  • Broader legal ecosystem. If your lease review leads to a demand letter, a dispute, or a need for your own documentation, Rocket Lawyer can help with all of it.
  • Document templates. Useful if you're negotiating and want to propose a counter-addendum or amendment in a professional format.

Cons

  • Doesn't read your lease. This is a significant limitation for the specific task of reviewing a lease you've received. Rocket Lawyer's tools are largely templating and consultation services — they don't ingest your document and explain it.
  • Cost. Attorney consultations and full platform access require a paid membership (typically around $9.99–$39.99/month depending on the plan) or per-consultation fees. For a one-time lease review, this can feel disproportionate.
  • Slower. Getting attorney feedback — even in the expedited formats Rocket Lawyer offers — takes time. If you have 24 hours to sign, this creates pressure.
  • Generic guides don't catch your specific traps. Reading "what to look for in a lease" articles won't flag the specific clause in your lease that auto-renews for 12 months with 60 days' notice.

Pricing Reality Check

TermPlainly is positioned as a low-cost document tool. For tenants doing a one-time lease review, the cost is modest compared to the alternative of paying a lawyer hourly.

Rocket Lawyer operates on a membership model. The free tier is limited. To actually consult an attorney or access the full document library, you'll need to subscribe or pay per service. For a single lease review, you could end up paying more than you'd expect unless you're already a member or plan to use the platform repeatedly.

Neither service replaces a local tenant rights organization, which is often free.

When Each Tool Is the Right Choice

Use TermPlainly if:

  • You have the actual lease document in hand and need to understand it quickly
  • You're a first-time renter who finds legal language genuinely confusing
  • You want to identify specific clauses to ask questions about before signing
  • You're on a tight timeline and need answers in minutes, not days
  • Cost is a concern and you just need clarity, not counsel

Use Rocket Lawyer if:

  • You've already identified a problem clause and want legal advice on whether it's enforceable
  • You're in an active dispute with your landlord and need documentation support
  • You want state-specific guidance on tenant rights beyond what's in your lease
  • You need to draft a counter-proposal, addendum, or formal letter to your landlord
  • You're willing to pay for professional-grade legal help and want a one-stop platform

Use both if:

This is actually a reasonable workflow. Run your lease through TermPlainly first to get a fast plain-English summary and flag anything that looks unusual. Then, if you find clauses that concern you, take those specific issues to Rocket Lawyer's attorney consultation — rather than paying for a full attorney read of a document you didn't understand in the first place. You'll use the attorney's time (and your money) much more efficiently.

The Honest Bottom Line

For the specific task of reviewing a lease you've received, TermPlainly is the more direct and practical tool. It does the one thing that actually needs doing: it tells you what your lease says in language you can understand.

Rocket Lawyer is a more powerful platform overall, and it's genuinely better if your situation has escalated beyond document comprehension into legal strategy or dispute territory. But for a tenant who just wants to understand what they're signing, it's an indirect path to a fairly simple goal.

Neither tool is a substitute for knowing your local tenant rights — check your city or state's tenant protection resources alongside whichever service you use.
