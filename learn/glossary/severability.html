severability — what it means in a contract

What it means

Severability is a contract provision (or legal doctrine) that allows the rest of an agreement to remain valid and enforceable even if one part of it is found to be illegal, unenforceable, or void. Essentially, a bad clause gets "severed" — cut out — without bringing down the entire contract. Courts generally favor this outcome because it preserves what the parties actually agreed to rather than forcing them to start over.

Most contracts include an explicit severability clause (sometimes called a "savings clause") that states this intent directly. Without one, courts in some jurisdictions may still apply the doctrine, but the outcome is less predictable. The clause removes ambiguity and signals that the parties want the contract to survive partial invalidity.

Why it matters

Severability comes up most often when a court strikes down a specific provision — a non-compete that's too broad, a penalty clause that violates local law, or a limitation-of-liability cap that's deemed unconscionable. Without severability language, the opposing party might argue that because one clause is void, the whole contract is void, leaving you with no agreement at all.

It's also critically relevant in employment agreements, software licenses, and consumer contracts, where regulators or courts frequently invalidate individual terms. Businesses drafting standard-form contracts across multiple jurisdictions especially rely on severability to ensure that a clause deemed unenforceable in one state doesn't collapse the agreement everywhere else.

Example

A staffing agency includes a non-compete clause preventing an employee from working in the industry for five years within 500 miles. A court finds that restriction unreasonably broad and strikes it. Because the contract contains a severability clause, the rest of the agreement — confidentiality obligations, intellectual property assignment, compensation terms — remains fully in force. Without that clause, the employee might have argued the entire contract was void.

Common confusions

Severability vs. reformation: Severability removes the offending clause entirely. Reformation is a different remedy where a court rewrites the clause to make it enforceable (e.g., narrowing a non-compete's geographic scope rather than eliminating it). Some severability clauses explicitly authorize courts to reform rather than just sever, so the exact wording matters.

Severability vs. waiver: Waiver is when a party voluntarily gives up a right under a contract. Severability deals with a clause being legally unenforceable, not a choice made by either party.

"The whole contract is void" assumption: People often think that if any part of a contract is bad, the whole thing falls apart. That's rarely true in modern law, especially with an explicit severability clause in place. Courts generally try to preserve as much of the parties' agreement as possible.
