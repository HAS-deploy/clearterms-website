right of first refusal — what it means in a contract

What it means

A right of first refusal (ROFR) is a contractual right that gives a specific party the opportunity to enter into a transaction before the owner can offer it to anyone else. If the owner decides to sell, transfer, or lease the asset, they must first offer it to the ROFR holder on the same terms a third party has proposed. The holder can then either accept those terms and complete the deal, or decline — at which point the owner is free to proceed with the outside party.

Unlike an option, a right of first refusal is not triggered by the holder's choice alone. It only activates when the owner decides to transact and receives a bona fide third-party offer.

Why it matters

ROFRs appear in a wide range of commercial and personal contexts:

  • Real estate – A tenant may hold a ROFR on the property they rent, so if the landlord decides to sell, the tenant gets to match the buyer's offer before the sale proceeds.
  • Shareholder agreements – Existing shareholders often hold ROFRs on shares another shareholder wants to sell, preventing outside parties from joining the company without consent.
  • Joint ventures and licensing – A partner may have a ROFR on acquiring the other party's stake or on a new licensing deal.
  • Publishing and entertainment – Authors or artists sometimes grant publishers or studios a ROFR on their next project.

The right protects the holder from being locked out of a deal involving something they have a vested interest in.

Example

A startup's shareholder agreement gives each existing investor a right of first refusal on any shares another investor wants to sell. Investor A finds a buyer willing to pay $50/share for their 10,000 shares. Before closing, Investor A must formally notify the other shareholders. They have 30 days to purchase those shares at $50/share under the same terms. If none of them exercise the right, Investor A can sell to the outside buyer.

Common confusions

ROFR vs. right of first offer (ROFO): A right of first offer requires the owner to offer the asset to the holder first, before seeking third-party offers — the holder names their price or terms. With a ROFR, the owner finds a third-party deal first, and the holder simply decides whether to match it. ROFO favors the owner (they learn the holder's ceiling); ROFR favors the holder (they see the market price first).

ROFR vs. option: An option lets the holder buy the asset at a pre-set price whenever they choose, regardless of whether the owner wants to sell. A ROFR only springs to life when the owner initiates a sale — the holder cannot force a transaction.

"Right of first refusal" vs. "right of first negotiation": A right of first negotiation only obliges the owner to negotiate with the holder before going elsewhere. It does not guarantee the holder a chance to match a final offer, making it a much weaker protection.
