lien waiver — what it means in a contract

What it means

A lien waiver is a document signed by a contractor, subcontractor, supplier, or other party giving up their right to file a mechanic's lien against a property in exchange for payment. A mechanic's lien is a legal claim that allows unpaid workers or suppliers to place a hold on a property title, which can block its sale or refinancing. By signing a lien waiver, the payee acknowledges receiving funds and surrenders that claim — at least to the extent specified in the document.

There are two main types: conditional and unconditional. A conditional waiver only takes effect once payment actually clears. An unconditional waiver takes effect immediately upon signing, regardless of whether the payment is actually received. There are also partial waivers (covering payment through a specific date) and final waivers (covering all amounts owed on a project).

Why it matters

Lien waivers come up constantly in construction projects. Property owners and general contractors typically require subcontractors and suppliers to sign them before or alongside issuing each payment. This protects the owner from a scenario where a general contractor is paid but fails to pay their subcontractors — who could otherwise lien the property even though the owner already paid. Many states have standardized lien waiver forms, and using the wrong form or signing an unconditional waiver before a check clears can leave a party with no recourse if payment bounces or is reversed.

Example

A tile subcontractor finishes work and submits an invoice for $18,000. The general contractor sends a check along with a conditional partial lien waiver. The subcontractor signs and returns the waiver, which becomes effective once the $18,000 clears the bank. Later, when the job is fully complete and final payment is made, the subcontractor signs a final unconditional lien waiver, releasing all remaining lien rights on the project.

Common confusions

  • Lien waiver vs. lien release: These terms are often used interchangeably, but technically a lien release discharges a lien that has already been filed, while a lien waiver prevents one from being filed in the first place.
  • Conditional vs. unconditional: People sometimes sign unconditional waivers assuming they work like conditional ones. They don't — an unconditional waiver immediately kills your lien rights even if the check later bounces.
  • Lien waiver vs. no-lien clause: A no-lien clause in a contract tries to waive lien rights upfront before any work begins; many states prohibit or limit these. A lien waiver is exchanged transaction-by-transaction in return for actual payment.
