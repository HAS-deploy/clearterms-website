letter of intent — what it means in a contract

What it means

A letter of intent (LOI) is a document that outlines the preliminary agreement between two parties before a formal contract is signed. It signals that both sides are serious about moving forward and summarizes the key terms they've tentatively agreed on — things like price, timeline, and scope. Most LOIs are non-binding, meaning neither party is legally obligated to complete the deal, though certain clauses within them (like confidentiality or exclusivity provisions) can be legally enforceable.

The non-binding nature is both the LOI's strength and its risk. It lets parties negotiate openly without full legal commitment, but it also means either side can walk away without penalty — unless the LOI explicitly makes specific terms binding.

Why it matters

LOIs come up frequently in business acquisitions, real estate transactions, partnership agreements, and large vendor contracts. In an M&A deal, an LOI locks in a purchase price and structure before expensive due diligence begins. In commercial real estate, it sets lease terms before lawyers draft the full lease. In employment, an offer letter functions similarly — outlining compensation before an employment contract is executed. Understanding what's binding and what isn't in an LOI can prevent costly misunderstandings if the deal falls through.

Example

A buyer wants to acquire a software company for $5 million. Before spending money on due diligence, lawyers, and accountants, both parties sign an LOI stating the proposed purchase price, payment structure, and a 60-day exclusivity window during which the seller agrees not to negotiate with other buyers. The exclusivity clause is binding; the purchase price is not. If due diligence reveals problems, the buyer can walk away from the $5 million figure — but the seller cannot shop the deal to competitors during those 60 days.

Common confusions

  • LOI vs. contract: An LOI is not a contract. It expresses intent, not obligation. A signed LOI does not mean a deal is done — it means the parties have agreed to try to reach a deal.
  • LOI vs. memorandum of understanding (MOU): These terms are often used interchangeably, and in practice they function similarly. An MOU tends to be used more in government, nonprofit, or international contexts, while LOIs are more common in commercial transactions. Neither is inherently more binding than the other.
  • Assuming the whole thing is non-binding: People often sign an LOI thinking none of it is enforceable. In reality, specific clauses — confidentiality, exclusivity, break-up fees, and governing law — are routinely written as binding, even when the rest of the document is not. Always read those provisions carefully.
