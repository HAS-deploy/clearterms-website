force majeure — what it means in a contract

What it means

Force majeure (French for "superior force") is a contract clause that excuses one or both parties from performing their obligations when an extraordinary event outside their control makes performance impossible or impractical. Classic examples include natural disasters, wars, pandemics, and government-ordered shutdowns. The clause typically lists qualifying events and spells out what happens next — whether the obligation is suspended, delayed, or terminated entirely.

Not every hardship qualifies. Courts and contracts generally require that the event be unforeseeable, beyond the party's control, and unavoidable even with reasonable precautions. Simply being unable to make a profit, or finding performance more expensive than expected, usually doesn't meet the threshold.

Why it matters

Force majeure clauses become critical during large-scale crises. COVID-19 triggered thousands of force majeure disputes over hotel bookings, supply contracts, construction timelines, and event venues. If the clause applies, the affected party is relieved of liability for non-performance during the qualifying event. If it doesn't apply — or if the contract has no such clause — that party may be in breach and face damages. The specific language matters enormously: a clause covering "epidemics" may succeed where one limited to "natural disasters" would not.

Force majeure also interacts with insurance, particularly business interruption policies, which often have their own requirements about what qualifies as a triggering event.

Example

A catering company signs a contract to provide food for a stadium concert. A hurricane causes the government to ban large public gatherings the week of the event. The catering contract contains a force majeure clause covering "acts of God, natural disasters, and government orders." The caterer invokes the clause, suspending its performance obligation without owing the concert organizer breach-of-contract damages. Both parties are released from their duties for the duration of the ban.

Common confusions

Force majeure vs. frustration of purpose. Frustration of purpose is a legal doctrine (not a contract clause) that applies when an event destroys the main reason a party entered the contract, even if performance is technically still possible. Force majeure is a drafted clause with specific triggering events; frustration is a common-law fallback when no such clause exists.

Force majeure vs. impossibility. Impossibility (or impracticability) is another legal doctrine covering situations where performance literally cannot happen. Force majeure is broader and contractually defined — it can cover events that make performance highly burdensome, not just strictly impossible, depending on how the clause is written.

Assuming it's automatic. Many contracts require the affected party to give written notice within a specific timeframe to invoke force majeure. Failing to provide timely notice can waive the right to rely on the clause entirely, even if the underlying event clearly qualifies.
