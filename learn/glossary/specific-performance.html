specific performance — what it means in a contract

What it means

Specific performance is a court order requiring a party to fulfill their contractual obligations exactly as promised, rather than simply paying money damages. It's an equitable remedy, meaning a judge grants it when monetary compensation would be inadequate to make the wronged party whole. Instead of asking "how much is this breach worth?", a court using specific performance asks "how do we make this contract happen?"

Because it forces someone to act, courts treat it as an extraordinary remedy and only grant it when the subject matter of the contract is unique or irreplaceable — situations where no amount of money can truly substitute for what was promised.

Why it matters

Specific performance comes up most often in real estate disputes, because every parcel of land is legally considered unique. If a seller backs out of a signed purchase agreement, the buyer can sue for specific performance to force the sale rather than just recover their deposit. It also arises in contracts involving rare artwork, one-of-a-kind collectibles, closely held company shares, or situations where a specific individual's services are involved (though courts are extremely reluctant to force personal service).

For anyone drafting or signing a contract, knowing that specific performance is possible raises the stakes considerably. A seller can't simply decide to breach and write a check — they may be compelled to complete the deal.

Example

A homeowner signs a purchase and sale agreement to sell their property for $600,000. Before closing, they receive a better offer and try to back out. The original buyer sues for specific performance. Because real estate is unique and money damages (the difference in purchase price, moving costs, etc.) don't fully compensate for losing that particular property, a court orders the seller to complete the transaction at the agreed price.

Common confusions

Specific performance vs. damages: Damages give the injured party money to compensate for the breach. Specific performance forces the actual contract to be carried out. They are alternatives — courts typically award one or the other, and damages are the default; specific performance requires showing money isn't enough.

Specific performance vs. injunction: An injunction usually orders someone to stop doing something (a negative command). Specific performance orders someone to do something (a positive command). A non-compete injunction prevents working for a competitor; specific performance would compel completing a sale.

When it's unavailable: Courts won't grant specific performance for personal service contracts — you can't be ordered to paint someone's portrait or perform in a concert. That would raise serious concerns about involuntary servitude. In those cases, an injunction preventing the person from performing elsewhere is sometimes used instead.
