joint and several liability — what it means in a contract

What it means

Joint and several liability is a legal rule that makes multiple parties each fully responsible for an entire obligation — not just their proportional share. If three people are jointly and severally liable for a $90,000 debt, the creditor can sue any one of them for the full $90,000, rather than being limited to $30,000 from each. The party who pays can then seek contribution from the others, but that's their problem to sort out — the creditor doesn't have to chase everyone down.

The rule exists to protect the party owed money or damages. It eliminates the risk that a creditor gets stuck with an uncollectable share because one defendant is broke, missing, or defunct.

Why it matters

This comes up constantly in business contracts, loan agreements, and litigation. In a partnership or multi-party guaranty, lenders routinely insist on joint and several liability so they can pursue whichever co-borrower or guarantor has the deepest pockets. In tort law, when multiple defendants cause a single injury, a plaintiff may recover the full judgment from any one of them. In commercial leases, if two businesses co-sign, the landlord can pursue either tenant for the entire rent. Whenever you're signing alongside other parties, this clause determines whether your exposure is capped at your share or extends to the whole amount.

Example

Two co-founders personally guarantee a $200,000 business loan with joint and several liability. The business fails. One founder has no assets; the other owns a house. The bank sues only the second founder for the full $200,000. That founder cannot tell the bank to first collect $100,000 from the other — they're on the hook for everything. They may later sue their co-founder for contribution, but the bank is already made whole.

Common confusions

Joint liability (without "several") means creditors must sue all parties together and each is only exposed to their proportional share — a meaningfully weaker protection for creditors.

Several liability alone means each party is responsible only for their own defined portion; a creditor cannot collect one person's share from another.

Contribution vs. liability — people sometimes assume that being able to seek reimbursement from co-debtors limits their initial exposure. It doesn't. Joint and several liability determines what the creditor can demand; contribution is a separate right between the debtors themselves, and it's only as good as the other party's ability to pay.
