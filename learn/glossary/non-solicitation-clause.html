non-solicitation clause — what it means in a contract

What it means

A non-solicitation clause is a contractual provision that restricts one party from actively recruiting or soliciting the other party's employees, clients, or customers. Most commonly found in employment agreements and business sale contracts, it prevents a departing employee from poaching colleagues or clients after they leave, or stops an acquiring company from cherry-picking talent from the business it just bought. Unlike a non-compete clause, it doesn't necessarily prevent someone from working in the same industry — it only limits who they can actively approach.

The restriction is typically time-limited (often 6–24 months) and geographically scoped, though geography matters less for client solicitation in a remote/digital world. Courts generally enforce these clauses more readily than non-competes because they're seen as narrower and more proportionate.

Why it matters

Non-solicitation clauses come up in several real-world situations. When an employee resigns or is terminated, the clause determines whether they can immediately recruit their former teammates or reach out to clients they personally managed. In mergers and acquisitions, the seller is often prohibited from approaching the business's customers after the deal closes. In partnership agreements and agency contracts, they protect the relationships either party built during the collaboration. Violating one can result in injunctive relief (a court order to stop), damages, or both — making them worth reading carefully before signing.

Example

A senior account manager leaves a marketing agency and joins a competitor. Her employment contract included a 12-month non-solicitation clause covering clients she worked with directly. Three months later, she emails her three largest former clients inviting them to move their business to her new employer. The agency can sue for breach of the clause, seeking damages equal to the revenue lost from those accounts and potentially a court injunction stopping further contact.

Common confusions

Non-solicitation vs. non-compete: A non-compete stops someone from working for a competitor at all. A non-solicitation only limits who they can actively contact — they can still take a job at a rival firm, they just can't go after specific people or clients. The two are often paired together but are legally distinct.

Solicitation vs. acceptance: These clauses typically prohibit active solicitation — reaching out, pitching, recruiting. If a former client independently contacts the ex-employee and switches without any prompting, that usually doesn't breach the clause. The line between "they reached out to me" and "I reached out to them" is often disputed and fact-specific.

Non-solicitation vs. confidentiality clause: A confidentiality clause protects information; a non-solicitation protects relationships. Someone can breach a non-solicitation without using any confidential information, and vice versa.
