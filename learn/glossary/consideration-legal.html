consideration (legal) — what it means in a contract

What it means

Consideration is something of value that each party gives to the other as part of a contract. It's the "why" behind an agreement — the price paid for a promise. For a contract to be legally binding, both sides must give consideration. This doesn't have to be money; it can be an action, a service, a promise to do something, or even a promise not to do something you'd otherwise have the right to do.

Consideration must be bargained for — meaning it's exchanged as part of the deal, not given as a gift. It doesn't have to be equal in value (courts generally won't second-guess whether someone struck a good deal), but it must be real. A promise to pay $1 for a house can be valid consideration; a promise based on something already done before the contract existed usually isn't.

Why it matters

Consideration is what separates a binding contract from a gift or a mere promise. If someone promises to give you their car but asks nothing in return, they can usually back out — there's no consideration on your side. This comes up constantly in business contracts, employment agreements, settlements, and loan documents. When a company asks an existing employee to sign a non-compete, courts often scrutinize whether the employee received anything new (like a raise or a bonus) in exchange — because continued employment alone may not count as fresh consideration in some jurisdictions.

Example

A homeowner agrees to pay a contractor $5,000 to renovate a kitchen. The homeowner's consideration is the $5,000 payment. The contractor's consideration is the promise to complete the work. Both sides are giving something, so the agreement is supported by consideration and can form a binding contract.

By contrast: a friend promises to give you concert tickets "just because." You give nothing in return. If they change their mind, you likely have no legal claim — there was no consideration to enforce.

Common confusions

Consideration vs. motive or reason. Why someone enters a contract is irrelevant; what matters is whether something of value was actually exchanged.

Consideration vs. adequacy. People often assume a contract is invalid if the deal seems unfair. In most cases, courts don't require consideration to be adequate (a fair market price), only that it exists.

Past consideration. If someone already did something before the contract was formed, that past act generally can't count as consideration for a new promise. "I'll pay you $500 for fixing my roof last month" is typically unenforceable as a contract because the work predates the promise.

Consideration vs. formality. In some jurisdictions, contracts signed as a deed (a formal signed and witnessed document) don't require consideration. This is an exception, not the rule.
