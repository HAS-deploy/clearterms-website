default clause — what it means in a contract

What it means

A default clause is a provision in a contract that defines what constitutes a "default" — that is, a failure by one party to meet their obligations — and specifies what happens next. It typically lists triggering events (such as missing a payment, breaching a representation, or becoming insolvent) and grants the non-defaulting party specific remedies, which often include the right to terminate the contract, accelerate payments, or demand immediate repayment of outstanding amounts.

Most default clauses distinguish between events that constitute automatic default and those that require a cure period — a window of time (commonly 30 days) in which the defaulting party can fix the problem before remedies kick in. The clause may also address cross-default provisions, which trigger a default under one agreement if the same party defaults under a separate agreement.

Why it matters

Default clauses appear in virtually every substantive commercial contract: loan agreements, leases, bonds, supply contracts, and service agreements. For lenders, a well-drafted default clause is the foundation of their enforcement rights — without it, they may have limited ability to act quickly when a borrower's financial position deteriorates. For borrowers or counterparties, the scope of the default clause determines how much risk they carry and how easily they can inadvertently trigger it. Overly broad default clauses — for example, those with no cure periods or that are triggered by minor technical breaches — can put a party in default for reasons unrelated to their actual ability to perform.

Example

A commercial loan agreement states that missing a scheduled interest payment by more than five business days is an event of default. If the borrower fails to pay, the lender sends a notice, a five-day cure period begins, and if no payment is made, the lender can declare the entire loan balance immediately due and payable. That entire mechanism — the trigger, the notice, the cure period, and the remedy — is the default clause in action.

Common confusions

Default clause vs. termination clause: A termination clause governs how and when parties can end a contract (sometimes for convenience, sometimes for cause). A default clause specifically addresses breach scenarios and remedies. Termination may be one of the remedies available after default, but the two provisions serve different purposes.

Default clause vs. penalty clause: A penalty clause imposes a financial punishment for breach. A default clause defines what a default is and what rights it triggers — it doesn't necessarily impose a penalty, and remedies under it (like acceleration) may be entirely enforceable where a pure penalty would not be.

"Default" vs. "breach": All defaults are breaches, but not all breaches are defaults. A contract may define default narrowly — only certain serious breaches qualify — leaving minor violations subject to standard breach-of-contract remedies rather than the more aggressive remedies the default clause unlocks.
