warranty disclaimer — what it means in a contract

What it means

A warranty disclaimer is a contractual clause in which a seller, licensor, or service provider explicitly states that it is not making certain promises about a product or service. Rather than guaranteeing that something will work, perform to a specific standard, or be free of defects, the party providing the disclaimer is saying: "You are accepting this as-is, and we make no representations beyond what is expressly stated." Disclaimers most commonly reject implied warranties—legal guarantees that arise automatically under statutes like the Uniform Commercial Code (UCC), such as the implied warranty of merchantability (that a product is fit for ordinary use) and the implied warranty of fitness for a particular purpose.

To be enforceable under the UCC, a disclaimer of implied warranties generally must be conspicuous—meaning it stands out visually, often written in capital letters or bold text. Some states impose additional requirements or limit how broadly warranties can be disclaimed in consumer transactions.

Why it matters

Warranty disclaimers appear constantly in software licenses, equipment sales, real estate "as-is" purchases, and service agreements. For businesses, a well-drafted disclaimer limits exposure to product liability and breach-of-warranty claims. For buyers and end users, it signals that if the product fails or underperforms, the legal remedies they might otherwise assume they have could be severely restricted or eliminated. Courts have thrown out disclaimers that were buried in fine print or written in a way that a reasonable person would miss.

Example

A software company sells an analytics tool and includes the following in its license agreement:

THE SOFTWARE IS PROVIDED "AS IS." THE COMPANY EXPRESSLY DISCLAIMS ALL WARRANTIES, EXPRESS OR IMPLIED, INCLUDING BUT NOT LIMITED TO THE IMPLIED WARRANTIES OF MERCHANTABILITY AND FITNESS FOR A PARTICULAR PURPOSE.

If the tool produces inaccurate reports and the customer suffers a financial loss, this clause would bar the customer from suing for breach of implied warranty—leaving them with little recourse unless they can show fraud or that an express warranty was made and broken.

Common confusions

  • Warranty disclaimer vs. limitation of liability: These are related but distinct. A disclaimer says "we make no promises." A limitation of liability clause says "if we are liable, damages are capped at a certain amount." Contracts often include both.
  • Disclaimer vs. indemnification clause: An indemnification clause shifts responsibility for third-party claims; a warranty disclaimer eliminates the underlying warranty obligation.
  • Implied vs. express warranty: A disclaimer most commonly targets implied warranties (which arise by law without being written down). Express warranties—specific written or spoken promises—can also be disclaimed, but only if the language clearly and directly conflicts with the promise; courts are skeptical of attempts to disclaim specific representations made elsewhere in the same contract.
