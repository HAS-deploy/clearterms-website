limitation of liability — what it means in a contract

What it means

A limitation of liability is a contractual clause that caps the maximum amount one party can recover from the other if something goes wrong. Rather than leaving damages open-ended, the clause sets a ceiling — often a fixed dollar amount, a multiple of fees paid, or the value of the contract itself. It does not eliminate responsibility entirely; it simply puts a boundary on financial exposure.

Most limitation of liability clauses also specify which types of losses are covered by the cap and which are excluded. Direct damages are usually subject to the limit, while certain categories — like gross negligence, fraud, or willful misconduct — are often carved out and left uncapped.

Why it matters

This clause appears in virtually every commercial contract: software agreements, service contracts, consulting engagements, vendor agreements, and construction contracts. For businesses, it is one of the most heavily negotiated provisions because it directly determines financial risk exposure. A vendor wants the cap as low as possible; a customer wants it high enough to make them whole if something goes seriously wrong.

The cap is often set at the amount paid under the contract in the prior 12 months. This makes sense for a vendor on a small contract but can be catastrophic for a customer if a system failure causes millions in downstream losses. Courts generally enforce these clauses, so what you sign matters.

Example

A company pays a software vendor $50,000 per year for a data management platform. The contract includes a limitation of liability capped at fees paid in the preceding 12 months. A data breach caused by the vendor's negligence results in $2 million in regulatory fines and lost business. Under the cap, the company can only recover $50,000 from the vendor — even though actual damages are far greater.

Common confusions

Limitation of liability vs. indemnification: An indemnification clause shifts liability for third-party claims (e.g., a lawsuit from a customer's customer). A limitation of liability caps what one contracting party owes the other. They operate differently and often interact — a well-drafted contract addresses how the cap applies, if at all, to indemnification obligations.

Limitation vs. exclusion of liability: An exclusion of liability eliminates responsibility for certain types of damages entirely (e.g., "no liability for consequential damages"). A limitation caps the amount recoverable but doesn't eliminate it. Contracts often use both: excluding certain damage types and capping what remains.

Thinking the cap covers everything: Many limitation clauses carve out specific situations — IP infringement, confidentiality breaches, death or personal injury — where the cap does not apply. Always read the exceptions; they can matter more than the cap itself.
