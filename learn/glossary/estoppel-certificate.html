estoppel certificate — what it means in a contract

What it means

An estoppel certificate is a signed document in which one party confirms certain facts about a contract or legal relationship as of a specific date. By signing it, that party is legally prevented ("estopped") from later claiming those facts were different. In real estate, it most often comes from a tenant who certifies the current status of their lease — confirming the rent amount, lease start and end dates, whether the landlord is in default, and whether any side agreements exist.

Why it matters

Estoppel certificates become critical during property sales, refinancings, and commercial loan transactions. A buyer or lender needs to know the true state of existing leases before committing money. Rather than taking the seller's word for it, they go directly to tenants and ask them to certify the lease details in writing. Once a tenant signs the certificate, they cannot later contradict those statements — for example, claiming the landlord owed them a rent credit they failed to mention. Many commercial leases include a clause requiring tenants to provide an estoppel certificate within a set number of days (often 10–30) upon the landlord's request, and failure to do so can be treated as a default or as the tenant's agreement that all stated facts are accurate.

Example

A landlord is selling an office building. The buyer's lender requires estoppel certificates from all tenants before closing. Tenant A signs a certificate confirming: monthly rent is $8,500, the lease runs through March 2027, no rent has been prepaid, and the landlord is not in default. Two months after closing, Tenant A cannot file a claim saying the landlord promised them three months of free rent that wasn't mentioned, because the signed certificate confirmed no such agreement existed.

Common confusions

Estoppel certificate vs. lease abstract: A lease abstract is a summary prepared internally by a landlord or their team pulling key terms out of a lease document. An estoppel certificate is prepared by the party receiving it (often the buyer or lender) and signed by the tenant, making it a binding certification — not just a summary.

Estoppel certificate vs. SNDA agreement: An SNDA (Subordination, Non-Disturbance, and Attornment agreement) governs what happens to a lease if a lender forecloses. These are often requested at the same time as an estoppel certificate, so they get bundled together in conversations, but they serve completely different purposes. The estoppel certifies current facts; the SNDA sets rules for a future event.

"Estoppel" as a general legal term: Estoppel has a broader meaning in law (preventing someone from contradicting a prior position they've taken). The certificate is a specific, practical tool that formalizes this concept in written, transactional form.
