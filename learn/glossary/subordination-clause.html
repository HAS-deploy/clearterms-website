subordination clause — what it means in a contract

What it means

A subordination clause is a provision in a loan or lease agreement that makes one party's rights or claims legally inferior to those of another party. In the mortgage context, it means a junior lender agrees that its lien on a property ranks below ("is subordinate to") the claim of a senior lender. In a commercial lease, it means the tenant agrees that the landlord's mortgage holder has priority over the tenant's leasehold interest if the property goes into foreclosure.

Subordination is often paired with an attornment clause, in which the tenant agrees to recognize a new landlord (such as a lender who forecloses) as their landlord. Together they appear in a single document called a SNDA (Subordination, Non-Disturbance, and Attornment Agreement).

Why it matters

This clause matters most when money is on the line and priorities need to be established:

  • Refinancing: A first mortgage lender may require a second mortgage holder to sign a subordination agreement to maintain first-lien position after a refinance.
  • Commercial leases: Landlords' lenders routinely require tenants to sign subordination clauses before a loan closes. Without it, a recorded lease could rank ahead of the mortgage, complicating the lender's foreclosure rights.
  • Bankruptcy: When a borrower or landlord files for bankruptcy, the order of subordination determines who gets paid first from any available assets or sale proceeds.

Failing to understand a subordination clause can mean a tenant loses their lease in foreclosure, or a junior lender recovers nothing if the property value doesn't cover the senior debt.

Example

A small business signs a 10-year lease for retail space. Before closing on a construction loan for the building, the bank requires the tenant to sign an SNDA. The subordination clause within it confirms that the bank's mortgage takes priority over the tenant's lease. In exchange, the non-disturbance clause protects the tenant: if the landlord defaults and the bank forecloses, the bank agrees not to terminate the lease as long as the tenant is current on rent.

Common confusions

  • Subordination vs. subrogation: These sound alike but are unrelated. Subrogation is when one party steps into another's legal shoes (e.g., an insurer suing on behalf of a policyholder). Subordination is strictly about priority ranking.
  • Automatic vs. voluntary subordination: Subordination can happen by operation of law (e.g., a second mortgage recorded after a first is automatically junior) or by signed agreement. Confusion arises when parties assume their position is fixed without realizing a signed clause can change it.
  • Subordination without non-disturbance: Tenants sometimes sign subordination clauses without negotiating for a non-disturbance provision, leaving them vulnerable to lease termination in foreclosure — a significant and commonly overlooked risk.
