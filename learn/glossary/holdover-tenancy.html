holdover tenancy — what it means in a contract

What it means

A holdover tenancy occurs when a tenant continues to occupy a rental property after their lease has expired, without signing a new lease — and the landlord has not yet taken steps to remove them. The tenant is technically staying without a current agreement, but their presence isn't automatically treated as trespassing. Instead, the law often converts the situation into a new, implied tenancy, typically on a month-to-month basis, under the same general terms as the expired lease.

The key factor is the landlord's response. If the landlord accepts rent from the holdover tenant, most jurisdictions interpret that as consent to a new rental period — often binding both parties to another full term in commercial leases, or a rolling monthly arrangement in residential ones. If the landlord does not accept rent and wants the tenant out, they can pursue eviction (sometimes called an "unlawful detainer" action).

Why it matters

Holdover tenancy comes up in several practical situations. Commercial tenants who are negotiating a new lease but haven't finalized it sometimes hold over intentionally, not realizing they may have accidentally locked themselves into another full year. Landlords who cash a rent check during this period can inadvertently waive their right to evict quickly. In residential settings, a holdover clause in the original lease may specify a penalty rent — often 125–200% of the standard monthly rate — to discourage tenants from staying beyond their term without notice.

For landlords, it's important to act decisively: communicate in writing that the holdover is not consented to, and avoid accepting payment if you intend to reclaim the property. For tenants, knowing whether your jurisdiction defaults to month-to-month or a full new term can significantly affect your financial exposure.

Example

A commercial tenant's five-year lease ends on December 31. Negotiations for a renewal stall, but the tenant stays in the space and pays January's rent. The landlord deposits the check without comment. In many states, this creates a new one-year tenancy under the original lease terms — meaning neither party can simply walk away until the following December without potential liability for breach.

Common confusions

Holdover vs. month-to-month tenancy: A month-to-month tenancy is a deliberate arrangement agreed upon by both parties. A holdover tenancy arises by default when a fixed-term lease expires without a new agreement — it becomes month-to-month only if the law or the original lease treats it that way.

Holdover vs. trespassing: Trespassing implies no prior legal right to be on the property. A holdover tenant had a valid lease; the legal question is what happens after it ends, not whether they had permission to be there originally.

Holdover vs. lease renewal: A renewal is a new, signed agreement. A holdover is an implied arrangement — no new document is executed, and its terms may be ambiguous or set by statute rather than negotiation.
