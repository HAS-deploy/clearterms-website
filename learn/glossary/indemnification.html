indemnification — what it means in a contract

What it means

Indemnification is a contractual obligation where one party agrees to compensate another for certain losses, damages, or legal claims. Essentially, Party A promises to "hold harmless" Party B if specific bad things happen — covering costs like legal fees, settlements, or judgments that arise from defined circumstances. The party making the promise is the indemnitor; the party being protected is the indemnitee.

The scope of indemnification is defined by the contract language itself. It can be narrow (covering only direct losses caused by one party's negligence) or extremely broad (covering any claim arising from the contract, regardless of fault). That scope is where most negotiations get contentious.

Why it matters

Indemnification clauses appear in almost every significant contract — vendor agreements, software licenses, construction contracts, employment agreements, and real estate leases. They determine who is financially responsible when something goes wrong. Without a clear indemnification clause, both parties may end up in expensive litigation just to figure out who owes what.

In practice, indemnification matters most when:

  • A vendor or contractor causes harm to a third party while performing services
  • A product injures an end user and the supply chain needs to sort out liability
  • An employee sues and both a staffing agency and the client company are named
  • A licensee's use of intellectual property triggers an infringement claim

Indemnification clauses are also heavily scrutinized by insurance teams, since they affect what coverage is required and who must be named on policies.

Example

A software company licenses its platform to a hospital. The contract includes an indemnification clause stating that the software company will indemnify the hospital against any third-party claims arising from defects in the software. When a patient sues the hospital alleging a billing error caused by a software bug, the software company — not the hospital — bears the legal defense costs and any resulting settlement.

Common confusions

Indemnification vs. limitation of liability: These often appear in the same contract but do different things. Limitation of liability caps how much a party can owe; indemnification determines who owes it. A clause can indemnify you while still capping the indemnitor's exposure.

Indemnification vs. insurance: Indemnification is a contractual promise between the parties. Insurance is a financial product that may (or may not) fund that promise. Contracts often require both — one party indemnifies and must carry insurance to back it up.

Mutual vs. one-way indemnification: Some contracts include mutual indemnification, where each party protects the other for their own wrongdoing. Others are one-sided. Assuming a clause is mutual when it isn't is a common and costly mistake.
