liquidated damages — what it means in a contract

What it means

Liquidated damages are a specific sum of money that parties agree in advance will be paid if one of them breaches the contract in a particular way. Rather than calculating actual losses after the fact, the contract locks in the amount upfront. For a liquidated damages clause to be enforceable, the pre-agreed sum must be a genuine estimate of the harm the breach would cause — not a punishment for bad behavior.

Courts will strike down a clause if it looks more like a penalty than a real attempt to estimate loss. The line between the two matters: penalties are generally unenforceable in common law jurisdictions, while true liquidated damages are not only enforceable but often preferred because they give both sides certainty.

Why it matters

Liquidated damages clauses appear most often in construction contracts, software development agreements, and any deal where delay or non-performance is hard to value precisely. A construction contract might specify $5,000 per day for every day a project runs past the completion date. Without such a clause, the party suffering the delay would need to prove actual losses in court — an expensive, uncertain process. The clause saves litigation costs and sets expectations clearly from day one.

They also show up in employment contracts (particularly non-competes or signing bonus clawbacks) and service-level agreements where downtime has a hard-to-quantify commercial impact.

Example

A software company hires a contractor to build a client portal, with a hard deadline of March 1. The contract includes a liquidated damages clause: $2,000 per day for each day delivery is late, up to a maximum of $30,000. The contractor delivers on March 16 — 15 days late. Rather than the client proving it lost $X in revenue during those two weeks, the contractor simply owes $30,000 (capped at the maximum). Both parties knew the stakes before signing.

Common confusions

Liquidated damages vs. penalties. People use these terms interchangeably, but legally they're different. A penalty clause is designed to punish or pressure compliance; a liquidated damages clause is designed to compensate. Courts ask whether the sum was a reasonable pre-estimate of loss — if it's wildly disproportionate, it's a penalty and may be void.

Liquidated damages vs. actual damages. Actual (or "unliquidated") damages are calculated after a breach based on real, provable losses. Liquidated damages replace that calculation with a pre-agreed figure. Importantly, in most contracts a liquidated damages clause is the exclusive remedy for the specified breach — meaning you generally can't also sue for your actual losses on top of it.

Caps vs. liquidated damages. A liability cap limits how much one party can ever owe; a liquidated damages clause specifies what is owed for a defined breach. They serve different purposes and can coexist in the same contract.
