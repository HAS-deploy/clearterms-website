memorandum of understanding — what it means in a contract

What it means

A memorandum of understanding (MOU) is a written document that records the agreed intentions or general terms between two or more parties before a formal contract is signed. It outlines what each party expects from the arrangement — goals, roles, timelines, and responsibilities — without creating legally binding obligations in most cases. Think of it as a handshake on paper: it signals serious intent and establishes a shared framework, but typically carries no enforceable consequences if a party walks away.

Whether an MOU is legally binding depends on its specific language and the jurisdiction. If the document includes clear consideration, definite terms, and language suggesting the parties intend to be bound, a court may treat it as enforceable despite the label. The name alone doesn't determine its legal weight.

Why it matters

MOUs appear frequently in business partnerships, government agreements, nonprofit collaborations, and international relations. They're used when parties want to formalize their understanding and move forward cooperatively — perhaps sharing resources or beginning joint work — before lawyers finalize a full contract. In mergers and acquisitions, an MOU (or letter of intent) often precedes the purchase agreement. In public sector contexts, government agencies use MOUs to coordinate responsibilities across departments or with outside organizations.

Because an MOU can inadvertently create binding obligations if drafted carelessly, it's important to review the language carefully. Clauses covering confidentiality, exclusivity, or dispute resolution that appear in an MOU can be enforceable even when the rest of the document is not.

Example

A software company and a hospital system want to explore building a patient data platform together. They sign an MOU stating that the company will provide technical resources and the hospital will share anonymized data for a six-month pilot, with both parties aiming to negotiate a full contract by year's end. Neither party is committed to completing the deal, but the MOU gives them a structured basis to start collaborating and signals mutual seriousness to their respective boards.

Common confusions

MOU vs. contract: A contract requires offer, acceptance, and consideration, and creates enforceable obligations. An MOU generally does not — but as noted, poorly worded MOUs can blur this line.

MOU vs. letter of intent (LOI): These terms are used almost interchangeably in practice. An LOI is slightly more common in commercial deals (especially M&A), while MOU appears more often in government or nonprofit contexts. The legal distinction is minimal; what matters is the actual language inside either document.

MOU vs. term sheet: A term sheet is typically narrower, focusing specifically on the key financial and structural terms of a deal. An MOU is broader and may cover the overall relationship, not just deal mechanics.
